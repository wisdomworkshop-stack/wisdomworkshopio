Art of Living Beautifully

Creative psychology meets timeless self-improvement

"The object is not to make art but to be in that wonderful state which makes art inevitable."

Robert Henri

Welcome. You've joined the waitlist for a five-week exploration of what the ancient philosophers — and the best of modern creative psychology — knew about flourishing. This isn't a lecture series. It's a practice: reading, writing, and gathering with a small group of people who take the question of how to live seriously enough to sit with it together.

Before We Begin

Read

  • The Art Spirit by Robert Henri — A painter's philosophy of the good life. Read the first 30 pages and underline everything that surprises you.
  • Letters from a Stoic by Seneca — Letters 1–5. Short, practical, 2,000 years old, still the best advice on attention and time you'll find.
  • Flourish by Martin Seligman — Chapter 1. The PERMA model as a scientific framework for well-being.

Write

Spend ten minutes with this prompt before our first gathering:

"What does a beautiful life look like to me — not the one I'm supposed to want, but the one that actually fits?"

Listen

This week, find a conversation with someone older than you — a parent, a mentor, a neighbor. Ask them: "What do you know now about living well that you didn't know at my age?" Bring what they say.

The Five Weeks

Each week, a different philosophical tradition meets a living practice: the Stoics on attention and equanimity. The Epicureans on friendship and simple pleasure. The Brahmaviharas — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity — as daily exercises. Aristotelian virtue ethics on character and flourishing. And creative psychology on the state that makes art inevitable. You'll leave with a personal philosophy of the good life — not borrowed, but composed.
