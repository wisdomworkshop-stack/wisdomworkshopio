Why we begin with Rogers

Before there is a method for holding a group, there is a person holding it. Rogers spent fifty years asking what that person needs to be, and his answer was disarmingly short.

He wrote A Way of Being at seventy-eight. By then he had stopped calling his work "client-centered therapy" and started calling it a person-centered approach: a stance that applies anywhere the growth of a person, a group, or a community is part of the point. That is our territory. You are not training to be therapists. You are learning to make rooms where people grow, and Rogers is the one who noticed that such rooms have a climate, and that the climate comes from you.

One caution he would want you to hear from the start. Rogers watched his ideas get flattened into a technique, the parrot's trick of repeating someone's last words back, and it bothered him enough that he went quiet about listening for years. So nothing in this guide is a script. Treat every exercise below as a way of finding out what gets between you and another person.

Key ideas

A climate, and a trust

Rogers's whole approach rests on one wager. Every living thing leans toward its own fuller form, the way those basement potatoes lean toward the light, and people are no exception. He called it the actualizing tendency. A facilitator does not install growth in anyone. A facilitator tends conditions and gets out of the way.

The conditions are three, and he insists they are attitudes you hold, which means they cannot be faked for long.

Congruence

Being real

What you feel, what you know you feel, and what you show line up. No professional front. In a circle: you say "I'm lost right now" when you are.

Prizing

Unconditional positive regard

A warm, non-possessive caring for the person as they are at this moment, with no strings about who they should become. In a circle: the difficult participant is still welcome.

Empathy

Understanding from inside

Sensing another's world as if it were yours, without losing the "as if," then checking whether you have it right. In a circle: you find out what they meant before you respond to what you heard.

One more thing, easy to miss: the other person has to receive it. Your care does not count until it lands. This is why Rogers keeps checking, and why the same is true of the regard we offer ourselves.

Field notes

The Listening Lab

A quiz on Rogers would test whether you can define congruence. That would miss the point rather exactly. The lab is a real conversation with a real person, in our circle and beyond it. Everything below is something to carry into one.

Before · know your default

What do you say first?

Six moments from a facilitator's life. Pick the response closest to what would actually come out of your mouth. Be honest; no one is grading. Rogers's colleague Elias Porter sorted helper responses into five kinds, and all of us have a favourite. Knowing yours is what you take into the room.

During · ten minutes, three chairs

The conversation

Find two people from the circle. One round is ten minutes of speaking and three of reflection; rotate so everyone sits in each chair. About forty minutes in all. On Zoom, the watcher turns their camera off.

Speaker

Talk about something that matters to you this week. It does not need to be heavy. Follow it where it goes. If the listener gets you wrong, say so; that is a gift to them.

Listener

No advice, no fixing, no questions that steer. Every so often, say back what you are hearing and let them correct you. Silence is allowed. It is usually someone thinking.

Watcher

Keep the time and stay out of it. Watch the speaker's face more than the listener's words. Notice the moment the speaker goes deeper, and the moment they pull back.

With one person, beyond the circle: skip the watcher. Ask a friend, a partner, or a colleague for ten minutes, tell them you are practicing listening, and trade places afterward if they are willing.

During · the listener's pocket card

Words to borrow, and words to hold

To say back what you heard. Offer it as a guess they can correct.

"I heard you say… is that what you meant?"

"It sounds like…"

"I'm not sure I have this right, but…"

"So part of you…, and another part…"

To notice in yourself and set down. None of these is wrong. They wait until the person feels heard.

"Have you tried…" (advising)

"I'm sure it'll be fine." (reassuring)

"That's probably because…" (interpreting)

"But why did you…" (probing)

"I know exactly what you mean. One time I…" (your story, in place of theirs)

A wrong guess is fine. It invites the other person to tell you what is actually true, and that correction is often where the conversation turns.

During · the watcher's tally

What kind of response was that?

Tap once each time the listener responds. You are counting, never scoring. Share the tally only if the listener asks for it.

After · three minutes together

Tell each other what happened

Speaker: When did you feel most heard? Was there a moment you closed up a little?

Listener: What did you hold back? What did it cost you, and what did it make room for?

Watcher: What did you see change in the speaker, and what came just before it?

Two more to carry beyond the circle

  1. The one ruleIn one real disagreement this week, before you make your point, restate the other person's point until they say "yes, that's it." Rogers proposed this in 1951 and predicted you would find it one of the hardest things you have ever tried. Notice what happens to your own position while you do it.
  2. Ask to be heardThe reverse practice, and the one facilitators skip. Ask someone to listen to you for ten minutes without fixing anything. You cannot offer a climate you have never stood in.

Process letter one

Your purpose, and the community you're building

A few places to begin. Follow whichever one has heat in it, and leave the rest.

Who heard you? Name a person who once listened to you in the way Rogers describes. What did they do, and what did they not do? What became possible afterward?

Who are you gathering, and what do you trust about them? If people really do lean toward their own growth, what are the people in your community leaning toward?

Which condition costs you the most? Being real, prizing without strings, or understanding before answering. Tell the story of a time it cost you.

To bring to the circle

What have you learned in your relationships with yourself and with others? Is there anything that worries you? What commitments would you like to make?
